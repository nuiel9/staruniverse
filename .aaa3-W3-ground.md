# W3 — the landed ground (src/world/Surface.js)

Everything below is a measurement of rendered pixels, taken with Playwright
against the running dev server on an Apple M4 through ANGLE/Metal. Nothing here
is an argument about what the shader ought to do.

Probes live in the scratchpad at
`/private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-krainats-staruniverse/da01630a-.../scratchpad/`:
`w3ground.mjs` (albedo statistics by real world distance), `w3ab2.mjs` /
`w3ab3.mjs` (paired, interleaved GPU timing).

---

## The harness, and why my first three numbers were worthless

Worth writing down, because the next agent will hit all three.

1. **The dusk probe never reached dusk.** The scratchpad's `fps.mjs` copies
   `judgeset.mjs`'s search for the hour but does not assert on the result, so a
   run that fails to move the sun silently measures the day frame twice — which
   is exactly what it did (day 5.1 fps, "dusk" 5.7). Every probe here calls
   `Game.setSunElevation()` and prints `uSunDir.y` beside every number. Day is
   0.34, dusk is 0.06.
2. **The engine's dynamic resolution was live.** A slow frame shrinks the render
   target, so the next measurement is of a different number of pixels — and an
   upscaled frame has a *lower* local sigma for reasons that have nothing to do
   with the albedo. Pinned the way `tools/gndperf.mjs` pins it, and the
   drawing-buffer size is printed beside every timing so a changed one cannot
   pass as a changed cost. (My first pinned attempt did not take: it reported
   2240x1260 for a 1600x900 viewport at dpr 1. `renderer.setPixelRatio(1)` on
   top of the engine fields is what fixed it.)
3. **Wall-clock frame rate on this box is a contention artefact.** Other agents
   are running probes on the same machine; the landed scene reported 5-7 fps at
   a GPU time of 24-36 ms, which is arithmetically impossible. Worse, a
   sequential bisection drifts by a factor of ten inside two minutes — my first
   one came back with *every* variant slower than the baseline it was removing
   work from. **Everything quoted below is paired and interleaved**: A and B
   alternate frame by frame and what is reported is the median of the per-pair
   ratio, which is invariant to any drift slower than one frame. That is the
   only design that produced repeatable numbers here.

I also broke the build once, in exactly the way the brief warns about: a
backtick inside a GLSL comment inside a JS template literal. `node
--input-type=module -e "import('.../Surface.js')"` catches it in about a second
— it parses *and* resolves — and that check now runs after every edit.

## (a) Materials

### What was measured

The judge's own `y-landed` pose, horizontal 8-pixel bands at eleven image rows.
The distance of each row is not guessed: a ray is cast from the camera through
the row and marched against the CPU height field (`surface.heightAt`), so the
metre figure is real. `hSpr` is the saturation-weighted circular standard
deviation of hue in degrees; `Ssd` is the saturation spread, the statistic
earlier waves used against reference photography; `locSd` is the sigma of a 3x3
high-pass.

A mean saturation of 0.72-0.76 across the whole near field, on one hue, with a
hue spread of 8-19 degrees. Reference photography of grassland runs a saturation
of roughly 0.35-0.55. 0.75 is not a meadow, it is baize, and the judge's words
for it are the same finding.

### The cause

Not the lighting and not the haze. The vegetation albedo varies at 290 m, 48 m
and 9 m and **nowhere else**. A foot-height crop of the near field is thirteen
metres of ground, so all three of those bands are constants across it, and the
only thing left modulating the sward was the scalar `(0.86 + 0.28*lum)` — a
seven per cent swing of value on one hue. Multiplying a colour by a scalar
preserves every hue ratio in it, so there was structurally no way to get
anything but one green at one chroma however hard the noise was driven.

This is the same structural fault the *mineral* block thirty lines above had
already diagnosed for itself and fixed with a chroma pivot ("Multiplying the
palette by a scalar preserves every hue ratio in it, so there was structurally
no way to get pale chips on red sand"). The sward never got one.

### What changed

All of it inside `if(uVeg > 0.01)`, so every desert, ice, barren, iron and lava
frame in the judge set is **bit-identical by construction** and none of the
previous waves' mineral tuning is touched.

* **A thatch pole.** A sward is live blade, last year's dead blade standing in
  the same tussock, and the litter between; the dead fraction is not a darker
  green, it is a different hue about forty degrees toward straw at roughly half
  the chroma. Mixing along that axis lowers the mean saturation and raises its
  spread at the same time, which is the pair of things that were wrong.
* **Driven off `tone`**, which is already in a register from the mineral pivot:
  the baked tile's per-clast mineral channel plus its luminance. A
  metre-and-finer field with a mip chain, so it cannot alias, and it costs **not
  one** additional texture fetch or noise octave — which matters, because this
  shader covers most of a landed frame and the file's own measurement is that a
  new per-fragment simplex here is worth about three milliseconds.
* **The bias is graded on the pixel footprint** (`nearK`, also already in a
  register). See the regression below.
* **Coverage thins at the scale you walk across.** `cov` was a constant near
  0.94 over the whole foreground, so the mineral ground — which has four
  separate bands of chromatic work done to it in the two hundred lines above —
  was painted out everywhere at once. The same tile channel that chooses thatch
  chooses where the litter and the trodden bare patches are (bare ground and
  dead matter go together; a bright chip in the tile is a stone the grass is not
  growing on), and the closed-sward ceiling comes down from 0.94 to 0.90.

### The intermediate version, and the regression it caused

First cut used a **flat** +0.16 bias. The near field did what it was asked and
the middle distance went with it, because `tone` goes to exactly 0.5 as the tile
minifies — that is the contract that makes every tile-driven term in this shader
fade invisibly — so a constant bias lands the far ground on a *fixed* thatch
weight with no variation left in it:

| dist | hue before | flat bias | graded bias |
|---|---|---|---|
| 428-702 m | 85.3 | 69.7 | 70.0 |
| 873 m | 88.0 | 70.8 | 71.5 |
| 964 m | 92.2 | 73.3 | 77.1 |
| 1023 m | 94.0 | 72.1 | 78.8 |

and the far-field **hue spread** fell from 19.4-19.6 to 17.1-18.2. A whole
landscape turned one flat straw is the same fault with the hue moved left.
Grading the bias from +0.20 at the boots to -0.22 at the horizon recovered it
and then some (far hue spread 21.2-22.3, *above* the before value), and there is
a real observation behind the grading: standing in a sward you look at the
**side** of the canopy, which is mostly stem and dead blade; from a kilometre
you look at the **top** of it, which is this year's live leaf, and that is why a
distant meadow is greener than the one you are standing in.

### The haze — measured, and deliberately not touched

I was ready to raise the dust column and did not, because the measurement says
it is already working. `update()` writes `uHazeH = 1800` and `uDustH = 250` (the
`70`/`1100` at construction is dead code), `uExtD = 1/2000`, `uExtR = 1/2200`
scaled by the atmosphere's own spectrum — and the *before* table already shows
saturation falling 0.76 → 0.37 and value sigma falling 39 → 19 between 49 m and
1 km. That is aerial perspective doing its job.

What made it unreadable is that the near field was pinned at the top of the
saturation range, so the veil had nothing to be a departure *from*. The mid
ground at 200-700 m now sits at 0.44-0.58 against a near field of 0.58-0.60,
where before it sat at 0.55-0.67 against 0.72-0.76: the planes separate because
the near one is no longer saturated to the ceiling. Those constants are also
shared with every desert frame in the set and were tuned by a previous wave
against a judge, so moving them to fix a green world would have been a trade I
could not measure the far side of.

**One honest regression:** far-field saturation *spread* is down (0.156-0.173 →
0.107-0.120). The thatch mix pulls toward a target derived from the sward's own
luminance, which compresses the differences the community terms had put there.
Relative to the (lower) mean it is nearly unchanged — Ssd/S goes 0.35 → 0.31 —
and far-field hue spread is up, so I judged it a net gain. It is the number to
watch if the far distance now reads flat.

## (b) Performance

**No performance change is in this diff, and that is a measured result rather
than a shortfall.** I designed one, measured the thing it was supposed to fix,
found the thing costs about four per cent of a frame, and did not ship it.

### The dusk penalty is real but smaller here than 2.6x

Paired, interleaved, 1280x720, dpr 1, GPU timer query, two independent sessions:

| | day (sun.y 0.34) | dusk (sun.y 0.06) | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| session 1 | 14.14 ms | 18.60 ms | **1.243** |
| session 2 | 16.66 ms | 19.55 ms | **1.220** |

So dusk costs about 22 per cent more, reproducibly, not 160 per cent. The
judge's 2.6x was measured at their resolution on their machine; whatever the
extra factor is, it is not visible at 1280x720 here. If the judge's frame was at
dpr 2 (5.7 Mpx against our 0.9), the gap is fragment-bound and scales with
pixels — which is testable and is the first thing I would hand the next agent.

### The mechanism the brief pointed at exists, and is not the cost

`fitGroundShadow` does grow the shadow box as the star drops. Read straight out
of `uShadowR` at each elevation:

| | fade-in radius | fade-out radius | PCF ground area |
|---|---|---|---|
| day | 133.5 m | 189.1 m | 1.00x |
| dusk | 172.8 m | 244.8 m | **1.68x** |

So 1.68x more ground runs the five-tap PCF at dusk. But it does not cost
anything worth having, and this is tested from **both** sides so that "the
branch was never skipped anyway" cannot masquerade as "the term is cheap":

| variant | day | dusk |
|---|---|---|
| PCF removed entirely (`uShadowR.y < 0`) | 0.973 | 1.012 |
| PCF forced over the **whole frame** (`uShadowR.y = 1e12`) | **1.043** | — |

Forcing the five-tap PCF onto every fragment in the frame costs 4.3 per cent.
The distance gate is therefore protecting about four per cent of a frame at
most, and the 1.68x growth of it at dusk is worth well under two.

(The mutation is applied *after* the game's own frame has written `uShadowR` and
undone immediately after the timed render. This matters: `uShadowR` is rewritten
every frame by `Game.fitGroundShadow`, which I verified at runtime, so a
one-shot poke in setup is either clobbered before the query opens or never
restored — and **both** failures land on a ratio of exactly 1.0, which reads as
"innocent". My first version of the probe had that bug.)

### The change I designed and did not ship

In the ground fragment shader, `sun = min(vShadow, smask)`. Where the marched
ridge shadow has already returned 0, `min(0, x)` is 0 for any `x`, so the PCF
lookup can be skipped outright — and dusk is exactly when most of the near
ground is inside a marched shadow, so the saving would land where it is wanted.
It is visually exact, it is in my file, and both call sites (the ground at
~4030 and the scatter at ~4590) would take it.

Per the measurement above it is worth at most one per cent, and a dynamic branch
around a texture lookup carries a divergence cost of its own. Shipping it would
have been an unmeasured change dressed as an optimisation, which is the failure
mode this brief exists to prevent.

### What the dusk cost is *not*

All paired and interleaved, at dusk:

| removed | ratio |
|---|---|
| the sky sphere | 0.991 |
| every instanced scatter mesh (rocks, tufts, trees) | 1.001 |
| the ground's PCF | 1.012 |

None of them. The atmosphere is analytic with no loops and no elevation-dependent
branch — I read it, and there is nothing in `SKY_FRAG` for a grazing sun to make
more expensive. The vertex-stage shadow march is *cheaper* at dusk, not dearer:
`sunShadow` breaks out of its eight steps as soon as `res <= 0`, and a nearly
horizontal ray goes underground far sooner than a steep one.

Hiding the ground at dusk made the frame **1.7x slower** (16.5 → 24.7 ms), so it
cannot be read as a subtraction: removing an occluder exposes the scatter and the
sky behind it. That is the one branch of the bisection I could not close.

### Where I would look next, and why it is not my file

By elimination the residual 22 per cent is in the ground program itself or in
the **shadow depth pre-pass**. The depth pass is the live suspect: `Game.js`
sets `shadowMap.needsUpdate` per frame in the landed scene, `fitGroundShadow`
grows the box and slides it up to 260 m down-sun as the star drops, and the
ground's custom depth material evaluates the height field per vertex. A larger
box at dusk means more of the world inside the shadow camera.

That lives in `Game.setShadowScale` / `Game.fitGroundShadow`, which is outside
my file list, so per the rules I am stopping and saying so rather than editing
it. `w3ab3.mjs` takes a new variant in four lines; the one to add is
`Object.defineProperty(renderer.shadowMap, 'needsUpdate', {get:()=>false,
set:()=>{}})`, paired at both elevations.

## What the judge should check

1. **`y-landed` and `z-landed-dusk`, near field.** Does the ground still read as
   a sheet? The colour statistics moved a long way; the *relief* did not.
2. **Khaki.** The thatch pivot moves the near-field hue from ~90 to ~80 degrees
   and the mid distance to ~70-79. The file's own comment at the community
   weights records that an earlier attempt "turned a garden into khaki with
   weeds on it". I believe this one is on the right side of that line — the
   after screenshot reads as a dry-season grassland rather than a lawn — but it
   is a judgement about art direction and it is the judgement I am least sure of.
3. **Any world with `uVeg == 0`.** Deserts, ice, barren, iron, lava should be
   pixel-identical. Everything I changed is inside `if(uVeg > 0.01)`.
4. **The far distance at 400 m to 1 km.** Saturation spread there is down about
   30 per cent (see the honest regression above), against a hue spread that is
   up about 10 per cent.
5. **The dusk frame rate on a quiet machine.** My 1.22x does not reproduce the
   judge's 2.6x, and the difference matters: if it is resolution-dependent the
   fix is fragment-side, and if it is not, the depth pass in `Game.js` is where
   to look.

## What I skipped, and why

* **Relief under the sward.** `locSd` across the near bands went 4.85 → 4.87 —
  nothing. The albedo work moved colour, not relief, and "no texture" is a
  relief complaint. The mechanism is the baked tile normal being scaled by
  `(1.0 - alive*0.80)`, a fifth of the strength that put real grain into the
  desert. The comment there argues, correctly, that the rock set's fracture and
  scree striation drew "a shredded, scratched surface right through the grass"
  at full strength — that argument wins on the *rock* tile. What is missing is a
  sward-appropriate band, which needs either a fifth baked tile (a grass canopy
  normal) or an analytic tussock field with a gradient, and the second costs a
  new per-fragment octave which this shader's own measurements price near three
  milliseconds. Neither fits inside a one-file edit without a cost measurement I
  did not have the runway to take. **This is the highest-leverage remaining item
  on the landed scene.**
* **Distant mountains: no strata, no scree, no erosion.** Untouched. The strata
  term already exists and is gated on `bare`, `slope` and a pixel-footprint
  limit for reasons the comments document at length (it is what an owner
  previously called "a weird banding artefact on the mountains"), so loosening
  it needs its own measurement of the moiré it was tightened to remove.
* **The aerial-perspective constants.** Measured as working; shared with the
  desert frames; left alone. See above.
